How do we choose where to look?

Humans make many eye movements throughout the day to inspect objects of interest with more detail. Since we move our eyes ~3 times per second, choosing where to look is one of the most frequent decisions the brain is faced with. Typical visual attention experiments aim to predict where attention is shifted by considering what makes specific positions informative. In a series of experiments, I have provided evidence that the intrinsic attentional costs of attentional shifts are minimized: When choosing between two potential targets, observers will choose the least intrinsically costly target, given equal rewards. From this, we have recently proposed an economic model of visual attention, where information gain is traded off against intrinsic attentional costs.

How does neural connectivity support cognition?

Neural connectivity is ubiqutous: neurons are densely connected through synapses, areas communicate through oscillations, and long-range functional connectivity is readily studied using fMRI. But how does neural connectivity across these spatiotemporal scales support cognition? In my postdoc, I am investigating this using multiple methods. We are currently focusing on examining whether and how neural connectivity may be rapidly altered to support working memory maintenance.

How are past and present goals represented in the brain?

Cognition is typically conceptualized as goal-driven. We can voluntarily control our cognitive abilities: We can freely imagine a tranquil coastal scene, choose where we attend, and relive our past at will. However, across many cognitive tasks, previous trial information lingers and strongly biases current behavior. This manifests in visual search (intertrial priming) and working memory (serial dependence). While these behavioral effects are well-documented, their neural underpinnings remain controversial. Using a diverse set of methods including EEG, neurophysiological animal recordings, and eye-tracking, I am studying how the brain holds on to the past, while simultaneously pursuing present goals.
